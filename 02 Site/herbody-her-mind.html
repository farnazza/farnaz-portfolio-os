Her Body, Her Mind

Designing a first service for women caring for someone with cancer using AI-assisted workflows as solo founder

Executive summary

Her Body, Her Mind offers creative and reflective workshops in Berlin for women caring for someone with cancer. This case study follows it from my own caregiving to a prototype website, ready for its first usability test.

Role
Founder and sole designer
Timeframe
Project started September 2025; one week in Design OS, from brief to test-ready prototype
Tools
Claude Code (Design OS) in VS Code, Google Meet, Apple Notes transcription, FigJam, Claude Design
Status
Working prototype website, ready for its first usability test

The problem

Women in Berlin who care in person for a loved one with cancer find it hard to switch off, and the support they come across is usually built for the patient, not for them. Both women I interviewed described it that way. One found a group that was “much more geared to people with cancer”.

01

Act 1: Listening

Before the design process: where this started

“The human side of the project came first: the real human contact, before all the technicality of the design process.”

It began at home

This project didn’t begin with a brief or a design tool. Last year, I was the primary caregiver for my mom, who had end-stage cancer.

I won’t share much about her here, but that experience is where this idea came from. Caring for someone you love through that time changes how you see things: what support looks like, what’s missing, and how alone you can feel even when you’re surrounded by people.

Listening first

From there, I kept thinking about how to support women going through something similar, so I started where it felt most natural: by listening.

When I kicked off the project in September 2025, I interviewed 3 women who were going through chemotherapy. I don’t share these interviews, for consent reasons. I shared questionnaires through a cancer support group in Berlin. I did guerrilla research and went where people were, for example to Yes Cancer, a cancer convention, where I talked to caregivers and listened to their stories. At home, I kept researching and watched videos of cancer survivors talking about what helped them heal.

A space for conversation

I also started an Instagram page where I talk about the emotional side of cancer, for both patients and caregivers.

It became a quiet space for conversation. People reached out, shared their stories, and told me what they were missing.

A sharing circle

In March 2026, I organized my first event: sound meditation, breathwork and a sharing circle, co-facilitated with a yoga teacher who wanted to contribute to the project.

Eight women came, three of them caregivers. Sitting in that circle, hearing what they shared and how they responded to the different practices, gave me some of the most valuable insights of the whole project. In the closing circle, the caregivers said they’d love a space where they feel held and don’t have to prove anything to anyone, and language came up as a big barrier.

Narrowing the focus

In June 2026, at a Charité event, I met women from Charité’s international cancer support groups.

Caregivers had asked them whether any community for caregivers existed. They knew of none, and they shared that one would be very helpful. That pushed me to look more closely at what caregivers actually need. Between that research and my own experience, I realized how little exists, especially for international caregivers, who told me there are few events or communities for them. So I decided to narrow my focus to women caregivers.

From listening to building

As a solo founder, I used AI-assisted design workflows to bring this vision to life and build something tangible, so I could test my hypothesis and validate my assumptions: do creative and reflective workshops help women navigate this time? Most of what I had heard came from people who had already chosen a space like mine, so this was something to test, not confirm.

The interviews, the synthesis of those insights, and the desk research all shaped the product. It offers different creative and reflective workshops and makes signing up as easy as possible, to test whether this is what she needs: a hands-on session nearby, without planning ahead, among people who understand, where she talks only if she wants to.

I ran the project in Design OS, my workspace in Claude Code, set up in VS Code with a rules file every step had to follow:

  • AI speeds up my thinking and challenges it, but every decision is mine.
  • Every claim about users is traced to a source and tagged Supported, Weak or Invented.
  • Transcripts are anonymized by me before any AI touches them.

I used the skills from the Design OS course, and built my own where I needed them, for example desk research where several agents work on independent tasks in parallel.

Research and insight

Desk research. I ran the desk research with a multi-agent skill I built in Claude Code: several agents worked on independent research tasks in parallel, and I checked every quote word for word against its source. I mapped what already exists in Berlin. Groups for relatives do exist, but every one I found is in German or doesn’t state a language, meets in person on weekday evenings, and asks for registration or a preliminary talk. Hospital support is framed around the patient, for “Angehörige des Patienten” (Charité). I found no drop-in space in Berlin that you can join without registering.

Interviews. I interviewed two women over Google Meet. Each received a consent form beforehand, with a separate consent for recording. I transcribed with Apple Notes instead of a third-party transcription tool: the data is sensitive, some tools’ privacy terms weren’t clear enough, and each extra tool would have needed its own consent, so I kept the number of tools as low as possible. I anonymized the transcripts myself before any AI saw them, then turned them into 51 observations, which I reviewed one by one against the transcript.

One woman cares for her husband now in Berlin. The other cared in the past in the UK, which is outside my criteria, so her points count as hypotheses for Berlin.

What it couldn’t tell me. Both women’s care is light, so how needs change with heavier care is still open.

The insight

What helps

What helps these women is getting out of their head: into their hands, a different place or company.

“getting you out of your head and into your body”

“you can’t relax, can you? So I had to kind of find things to do”

What gets in the way

But the support around them is built for the patient, in German, on weekday evenings, so what helps is getting harder to reach:

  • Built for the patient
  • In German
  • Weekday evenings

“going out in the evening is just hard”

  1. 03 · Define

    9 How Might We questions

    I clustered themes in FigJam; AI audited my clusters against the data. Insights, need statements, problem frame, 9 HMWs, 1 proto-persona, success statements.

    Why this way: The audit checked my clusters, so I didn’t just find what I expected.

    FigJam, Claude Code skills

Act 3: The test

From concept to prototype

Which concept, and why

I generated five concepts through five lenses and audited them for diversity instead of ranking them. Two turned out to be the same behavior in different forms, and one rested on one participant and conflicted with the other’s needs. I chose “Come as you are, hands first”: a hands-on session nearby, without planning ahead, among people who understand, talking only if she wants to. It is also the concept closest to my starting hypothesis, so I designed it as a test of that hypothesis.

  1. 04 · Develop

    5 concepts, by 5 agents

    5 concepts by 5 AI agents in parallel, one lens each, audited for diversity. MVP scope, user flow, flow audit, edge cases, screen map.

    Why this way: Parallel agents gave different directions; the audit flagged the 2 closest to my own idea.

    Claude Code, FigJam

Decisions as hypotheses

Talking is optional; leaving early is fine

Why
Talking helped one participant; the other valued not having to talk
What the test will tell me
“What was it like being there with the others?”

No online payment, no price choice on the form; she pays on the day, in private

Why
Choosing the reduced tier online means telling a website she can only afford less; it also keeps payment data and a third party off the site
What the test will tell me
How she would prefer to pay

First name and email only, and “Reserve your place”

Why
A workshop booking could count as health data, so I asked for the minimum; “Reserve” makes clear nothing is paid now
What the test will tell me
Whether signing up at all is a barrier

“Caring for someone with cancer?”, not “caregiver”

Why
Many women don’t call themselves carers, and most cancer services speak to the patient first
What the test will tell me
“Who do you think this is for?”

In person only, close to where she is

Why
Being with others is part of what I’m testing. One woman’s hardest moment was having nobody nearby: “there was nobody who I could phone saying, let’s go and meet for a drink”. Online added too much complexity for a first iteration
What the test will tell me
Whether being with others helps: “What was it like being there with the others?” Whether it is within her reach: I watch for signs of effort or tiredness during set-up and during the activity, and ask “What would make it hard for you to do this again next week?” and “How did it fit with how much energy you had today?”

What’s next

The prototype is ready for its first think-aloud usability test, with new testers. I want to find out whether she:

  • gets absorbed in the activity, out of her head
  • sees it fitting her own moment
  • feels it costs no more energy or travel than she has
  • feels comfortable being there with others
  • can tell from the website which session suits her
